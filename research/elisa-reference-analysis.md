## 1. The three big videos

### 1a. BuzzFeed Unsolved (30M): "body first, then: how is that even possible?"

| % | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | Setting: we are driving to the hotel; "one of the most bizarre mysteries". |
| **~9** | **Discovery, flat and early:** checked in Jan 26, found Feb 19 in a rooftop water tank. |
| 13 | Rhetorical question to the co-host about what led to her discovery, answered: guests complained about low water pressure; water came out dark and tasted odd. (The discovery's *cause* is used as a second shock.) |
| 20 | Character detail: moved out of a shared room over "odd behaviour". |
| 25–40 | Elevator footage, reacted to live ("why are the doors still open?"). |
| 41–50 | Theories one at a time, each killed by a fact: drugs/drunk → toxicology clean; murder → no trauma. |
| **50–60** | **The escalation peak:** the roof route listed as options (fire escape via a window / alarmed door, no alarm heard), then a cumulative chain of steps she'd have to perform (platform, 10-ft ladder, 20-lb lid, get in, close the lid, clothes in the tank). The mystery gets *harder* the longer he talks. |
| 66–90 | Ghost theory + hotel history, played as a sceptic-vs-believer argument. |
| 95 | Ruling: accidental drowning, one sentence. |
| 96–100 | **Ending:** the real tragedy is the family (wrongful-death suit dismissed). One emotional note. |

- **Discovery lands at ~9%.** The body is the premise, not the climax.
- **Ending:** one ending, human, not a twist. The ruling is a single throwaway line just before it.
- **Uncertain facts in the voice:** hedges used sparingly and only on second-hand colour ("apparently", "reportedly", "according to some"). Core facts (date found, tank, toxicology clean) are stated flat. Theories are voiced as "another theory was that…" and then immediately tested against one fact.
- **Devices:** direct address (to the co-host = to us), the question-then-answer ("you want to know what it was that led to her discovery?"), lettered options (A / B), a cumulative list that builds impossibility, and a sceptic who closes each theory.
- Example phrase: *"you want to know what it was that led to her discovery"*.

### 1b. Caitlin Doughty (1.6M): "procedural cold open, a cascade of questions, then facts answer them one by one"

| % | Beat |
|---|---|

| 15 | Four-question cascade: how did she get to the roof? why? foul play? what about the elevator video? |
| 18–27 | Humanises her (student, art, fashion, a blog; lived with anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder) and states her stance: not an expert opinion. |
| 27–40 | "The basics we know": student, the hotel's history (briefly, with a joke), check-in, shared-room move, called her parents every day, then silence. |
| 40–58 | Elevator video: described precisely, then the door mystery given three explanations ranked by likelihood. |
| 60–67 | Found Feb 19 (restated), coroner delay, ruling: accidental drowning with bipolar disorder contributing; meds taken; no drugs or alcohol. |
| 69–80 | The big question: the roof. "Originally thought" only via an alarmed door → "has since emerged" there is a fire escape without an alarm. |
| **81–85** | **Hatch dispute:** it was originally thought the lid was closed (so how could she close it from inside?) → a much later interview with the worker revealed it was open, *meaning* she could have got in herself. |
| 86 | Judge dismisses the parents' suit. |
| 90–98 | **Ending:** her plain-spoken theory (young woman in a mental-health crisis climbs up, gets in, can't get out) + the family who still live with the questions. Then a comments CTA. |

- **Discovery at ~10%.** Every later section answers one of the four questions she raised at 15%.
- **Ending:** one ending (her realistic answer, then the family). The CTA is a channel habit, not the story ending.
- **Uncertain facts:** a consistent **correction pattern**: "It was originally thought X. It has since emerged / a later interview revealed Y, meaning Z." The disputed claim is *used* (it creates the impossibility) and then *resolved* by the person who was there. Speculation is labelled once ("some speculate", "others say") and she ranks it ("the most likely scenario").
- **Devices:** the procedural "then he climbed… then he climbed…" cold open (time slows before the find), a spoken question cascade, "here are the basics we know" as a reset, one thesis stated plainly near the end, humour to release tension.
- Example phrase: *"her discovery only raised more questions"*.

### 1c. Draw My Life (2.8M): "chronological, but the discovery is a turn, not a climax"

| % | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0–6 | Framing promise: one of the most mysterious cases; to some, "the perfect crime". |
| 6–27 | Her: 21, travelling alone, wanted a change, called her parents every night; they stop hearing from her; police search; posters; "not a single clue". |
| 27–35 | Turn: "…until" the elevator footage (buttons, in and out, talking to someone unseen, doors not closing). |
| 35–41 | The water complaint leads detectives up; muddy, weird-tasting water. |
| **41** | **Discovery.** |
| 41–52 | Facts that make it *worse*: no trauma, no drugs, drowned in tank water; bipolar "but that didn't explain how she ended up there"; restricted roof; heavy cover. |
| **52** | **The pivot question, spoken out loud:** how could she have got herself inside the tank? |
| 55–93 | Theories (hotel history, ghosts, a horror film, an elevator ritual, TV shows). Weak section: repeated paragraph, several wrong facts. |
| 96–100 | **Ending:** years later nobody knows what happened; it remains unsolved. Then a CTA. |

- **Discovery at ~41%**, but it is immediately followed by *escalation* (each new fact removes an easy explanation) and a spoken question. The second half is driven by the question, not by explaining the answer.
- **Ending:** one ending, the open question.
- **Uncertain facts:** "it seemed like", "for some people", "other theories talk about", "maybe". Hedges sit on theories, never on the events.
- **Devices:** "There wasn't a single clue, until…", the "So… how is it possible?" pivot, a promise at the top ("perfect crime") that the rest pays off, direct address to viewers at the end.
- Example phrase: *"There wasn't a single clue, until"*.

### What all three share

1. **The body in the tank is the premise, not the payoff.** Two of three reveal it in the first 10%; the third reveals it at 41% and immediately turns it into a harder question.
2. **After the discovery, the mystery gets bigger before it gets smaller.** Roof alarm, ladder, heavy lid, "closed" lid: stacked impossibilities. Only then does any explanation arrive.
3. **The driving question is said out loud** (BuzzFeed: how did she even get up there; Doughty: four questions; DML: "how is it possible?").
4. **Explanations come after the impossibility and are framed as answers to it.** Doughty's "originally thought / later revealed / meaning" is the cleanest version.
5. **Hedges only on theories and second-hand colour, once each. Events are stated flat.**
6. **Exactly one ending**: family (BuzzFeed), realistic answer + family (Doughty), unsolved (DML). The ruling is always a one-line passing fact, never the ending.

## 2. The two tiny shorts (no views): what they do badly

**Spinetinglers (110 s, 385 views)**
- Opens on the *hotel's* reputation (murders, Ramirez, Black Dahlia), not on her or on anything happening. The first 20% is a list.
- She has no character: "the body of a 20-something woman", later "the missing tourist". No reason to care.
- Several wrong facts (at the bottom of the tank, the lid closed on her).
- Vague attribution as a hedge: "you see, many question", "lots of people believe". Nobody specific believes anything.
- No escalation: the discovery (46%) is followed by an autopsy line, then straight to "how?" and a list of theories.
- Ends on a list of options + "let me know your theories in the comments".

**Fast and Felonious (43 s, 4 views)**
- Opens on the elevator (a good image), but says she "vanishes" (wrong) and then spoils her death at ~20%.
- Then calls the elevator footage "the real mystery", which downgrades the discovery it just gave away and replays a beat we already saw. Circular structure.
- Stacked flat claims at the end: roof locked. Lid too heavy. Ruled an accident. But nobody truly knows. What do you think? That is **four endings in 12 words**, and two of the claims are wrong.
- No character, no causal links ("so", "but", "until"): just statements.

**Does v22 share these faults?**
- *No* on character and accuracy: v22 has her (21, buying gifts, the parents) and is carefully sourced. That is its strength; keep it.
- **Yes on the ending.** v22 b10–b11 ("It was ruled an accident. How she reached that roof was never determined. That trickling tap is what sent a worker up to her. The girl buying gifts for her family.") has the same shape as Fast and Felonious's close: ruling → open question → something else → something else. Stacked declaratives, each a possible ending.
- **Partly on attribution-as-hedge**: "the lead detective believed", "may have been", "In his view", "A police spokesman believed", "their own detective agreed" is five attribution/hedge markers in three consecutive beats (b07–b09). The tiny shorts hedge vaguely; we hedge precisely but constantly. Both make the voice sound unsure.
- **Partly on "no causal links"**: b07–b10 are a chain of separate statements with no "but", "so" or "until" connecting them to a question the viewer is holding.

**Root cause:** v22 is built as a *withheld reveal* (tap → she's missing → search → she's there → explain). The references are built as a *premise → escalating question → answer* shape. With the text hook already spoiling the body, only the second shape works. The fix is structural, not line edits: move the find to the front, spend the middle making it impossible, spend 15 s on the answer, end once.

## 4. Recommended beat structure (target 66–72 s, ~165–180 words)

**Thesis (one sentence):** A 21-year-old vanished inside a busy hotel and was found in the water its guests had been drinking, and the detail that makes it look impossible (how she got in) has a quieter, sadder answer than the internet wants.

**Tense:** past throughout. **Hedge budget:** one "police believe" + at most one "early reports said". Everything else flat.

| # | ~sec | Purpose | Content (illustrative wording only: rewrite in our voice) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Hook / discovery | 0–7 | Deliver the premise immediately; matches the on-screen hook | Guests complain the tap water trickles and tastes wrong. A worker climbs to the roof, finds a tank hatch open, looks in. Elisa Lam, missing for 19 days, was inside. **Discovery at ~6 s (≈9%).** Procedural, short sentences, time slowing (Doughty's shape, our words). |
| 2 The question | 7–10 | Say the driving question out loud, once | e.g. "So how does a hotel guest end up inside a sealed tank on the roof?" (one question, not four). |
| 3 Her | 10–17 | Make us care before the mystery grows | Jan 31: 21, travelling alone, buying gifts for her family; her parents stop hearing from her. |
| 4 Escalation 1: the elevator | 17–27 | Mystery grows: her state of mind | That night: peers out, hides in the corner, presses every button, doors stay open, no one comes. |
| 5 Escalation 2: the search | 27–35 | Mystery grows: the dramatic irony | Dogs searched even the roof, never caught her scent. Nobody opened the tanks. Guests kept drinking the water. |
| 6 Escalation 3: the impossibility | 35–44 | Peak of the question (~60%) | Roof door alarmed, no alarm heard; a ladder up the tank; a heavy lid; early reports said the lid was shut. *How do you close a lid from inside?* This is where the hatch dispute *lives*: as the strongest reason the case looks impossible. |
| 7 The answer | 44–60 | Resolve the impossibility, once, plainly | Resolve the lid first: the man who found her said, under oath, it was open (and the lead detective agreed). Then "Police believe…" **once**: she had bipolar disorder and had stopped taking most of her medication; she climbed in herself while the tank was full; every tap guests ran lowered the water until she couldn't reach the top. Ruling folded in here as a clause ("ruled an accident"), not a separate beat. **Answer at 65–85%.** |
| 8 The ending (ONE) | 60–68 | One last line that reframes the hook | Pick exactly one. Recommended: tie the answer back to the premise: the tap they complained about was the water she was trapped in; it was also the only thing that brought anyone up to her. *Or*, if the owner prefers the open question: "No one has ever explained how she reached that roof." Not both. No name/dates fragment after it (dates go on screen). |

Why this ordering works for the owner's complaint:
- The on-screen hook and the voice finally agree (both reveal the tank at the top).
- The middle is *rising* tension (elevator → search → impossible lid) instead of a wait for a reveal the viewer already has.
- The explanation arrives as a relief to a question the viewer has been holding for 35 s, instead of 30 s of deflation after a climax.
- The trickling-tap callback moves from being a fourth ending to being the natural ending.

**How to voice the detective's theory:** attribute at the head of the beat, then tell it as narrative. Pattern: "Police believe [X]. [Y]. [Z]." No "in his view", no "may have been", no second attribution. The on-screen tag ("LEAD DETECTIVE'S OPINION · deposition") carries the legal precision; the voice carries the story.

**How to voice the hatch dispute:** as the correction pattern, positioned *before* the theory: "Early reports said the lid was closed. The man who found her said it was open, and under oath the lead detective agreed." That turns a side argument into the hinge that makes the theory possible. Fact-check note for the researcher: "early reports said closed" must be backed by a sourced report (we currently hold only the Den of Geek account of a spokesman's on-camera belief); if we can't source it as "reports", use "At first, police said it was closed" only if that is what the source supports, and keep "reportedly" on screen, not in the voice.

## 5. Rules to add to the script-writer

1. **Premise first.** If the hook text or title reveals the central fact (e.g. the body in the tank), the voice states it within the first 10 s. Never withhold in the voice what the screen already told the viewer.
2. **Say the driving question out loud, once, right after the premise.** One question, phrased as a question. Everything after it either raises the stakes of that question or answers it.
3. **Escalate before you explain.** Between the question and the answer, at least two beats must make the mystery *harder* (each removes an easy explanation). The answer starts no earlier than ~60% of runtime and is finished by ~85%.
4. **Attribute once, then narrate.** An opinion or theory gets one attribution at the start of its beat ("Police believe…"), then plain declarative sentences. Max two hedge/attribution markers in the whole script; legal precision (reportedly, opinion, deposition page) goes on screen.
5. **Disputed facts use the correction pattern and serve the arc:** "At first, X was said. Then [the person who was there] said Y." Place the disputed version where it raises the stakes, and the correction where it enables the answer. Never stage a dispute as a standalone gotcha after the explanation.
6. **Exactly one ending.** The final beat is a single idea: either the callback, the open question or the human note. The ruling is a clause inside the answer beat. No trailing fragments, no "what do you think?".
7. **One tense for the whole story** (default: past). No switch from present to reported at the climax.
8. **Link every beat to the previous one with an implicit "but", "so" or "until".** If two consecutive beats would read naturally joined by "and then" / "also", merge or cut one (the tiny shorts fail exactly here).
