# The Elisa Lam Case

[VISUAL: 05_elisa_lam_water_tank.jpg | 04_cecil_hotel_exterior.jpg]
For nineteen days, guests at a Los Angeles hotel drank from their taps, cooked, and showered — while a young woman's body decomposed in the rooftop water tank directly above them. The discolored, foul-smelling water was the first sign anything was wrong.

[VISUAL: 08_nbc_news_body_found.mp4]
Maintenance worker Santiago Lopez climbed to the roof on February 19th, 2013 and found Elisa Lam floating face-up in one of four cylindrical tanks — naked, twelve inches from the top. Her clothing and room key were in the tank with her.

[VISUAL: 02_elisa_lam_portrait.png | 06_elisa_lam_graduation.jpg | 03_elisa_lam_student_photo.jpg]
Elisa was 21, a University of British Columbia student from Vancouver traveling solo along the California coast. She blogged openly about her bipolar disorder and called her parents every day she was away.

[VISUAL: 07_cecil_hotel_los_angeles.jpg | 14_cecil_hotel_cbs_exterior.jpg]
She had checked into the Cecil Hotel — sixteen documented non-natural deaths, once home to serial killer Richard Ramirez, rebranded as "Stay on Main" to attract budget travelers. The new name hadn't changed what it was.

[VISUAL: 01_elevator_surveillance_footage.mp4]
Three weeks earlier, the LAPD released this footage to find her. Instead, it went viral. She presses every floor button, peers around the door frame, makes fluid gestures toward something no one else can see — and the doors refuse to close.

[VISUAL: 10_elevator_cctv_archive.mp4]
Police said the tank hatch was found closed. The maintenance worker who opened it said it was already open. That contradiction has never been resolved — and the LAPD had searched the hotel with dogs multiple times, never once checking the tanks fifteen floors above.

[VISUAL: 11_elevator_surveillance_still.jpg | 12_elevator_hiding_still.jpg | 09_elisa_lam_lapd_photo.jpg | 13_elisa_lam_cbs_portrait.jpg]
The coroner ruled accidental drowning. But while investigators were searching for Elisa Lam, a tuberculosis outbreak near the Cecil Hotel was being diagnosed using a test commercially called LAM-ELISA. That coincidence is completely real — and it explains absolutely nothing.
